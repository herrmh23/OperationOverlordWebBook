Historical-Fiction Diary
June 6, 1944

Dear Diary,

I was up before daylight this morning, same as always. There were cows to milk and breakfast to get on the table. I had the radio playing low in the kitchen when the announcer broke in with the news.

Our boys have landed in France.

Normandy, he said.

I stood there with the dish towel in my hands and forgot all about the biscuits in the oven.

Ever since Brock left, I have tried not to let my mind run away with me. There is no sense worrying over what you cannot change. There is work to do here, and Brock has his work to do wherever the Army has sent him.

But this morning was different.

I kept thinking about all those mothers listening to the same radio and wondering the same thing I was wondering.

Is my boy there?

I do not know where Brock is today. Maybe that is for the best. The Army cannot tell every mother where her son is, especially now.

I still remember when his notice came.

He sat at the kitchen table and read it twice before he said anything.

“Well, Ma,” he finally said, “looks like they need me.”

He tried to smile when he said it.

I tried to smile back.

I told him I was proud of him, and I was. I still am. But being proud of your boy does not keep you from wanting to hold on to him.

Before he left, I told him to write whenever he could, keep his head down, and remember everything his father taught him about looking after himself.

Then I hugged him harder than I meant to.

He laughed a little and said, “Ma, I’m coming home.”

I told him he had better.

After the news this morning, I turned the radio off and went outside.

Funny thing about a farm. The world can be at war, and the cows still need milking. The garden still needs tending. Chickens still scratch around the yard as if nothing has happened.

I walked out toward the field where Brock has worked beside me more times than I can count.

Everything looked just the same.

That almost made it harder.

Somewhere across the ocean, boys who worked farms and fixed fences and complained about getting up early are carrying rifles instead of pitchforks.

And their mothers are waiting.

I suppose that is what I will do too.

Wait. Work. Listen to the radio.

And pray.

Tonight I will turn the radio on again after supper. I will listen for whatever news they can give us. Then I will say my prayers and get up tomorrow morning to do the work that needs doing.

That is about all a person can do.

Please, Lord, watch over Brock.

Bring my boy home.

Mary

Text Evidence vs. Historical Context

What Comes from the Story—and What Comes from History?

Mary’s diary helps you examine a home-front perspective. Outside historical sources help you verify the larger events and circumstances surrounding D-Day.

Text Evidence

What Mary says inside the diary

Mary is a farm mother. She hears news of the Normandy invasion on the radio, does not know where Brock is, remembers his departure, continues her farm chores, worries about him, and turns to prayer while she waits for more news.

Historical Context

What verified outside information establishes

June 6, 1944 was D-Day, when Allied forces began the Normandy invasion. Americans heard early-morning radio bulletins and continued receiving invasion reports throughout the day. Wartime families often had to live with delayed or incomplete information about relatives serving overseas.

Perspective limit: Mary’s diary can show how one mother might experience waiting, worry, work, and radio news. It cannot tell us the full story of D-Day or stand for every family on the home front.
