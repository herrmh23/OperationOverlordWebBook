Opening Historical Context

One Person Inside a Vast War

On June 6, 1944, Otto Frank and the seven other people hiding in the Secret Annex were still concealed in Nazi-occupied Amsterdam. The Allied landings in Normandy brought real hope, but they did not end the danger surrounding them.

The Anne Frank House documents that after the landings, Otto followed the Allied advance by placing pins in a small map. Less than two months later, on August 4, the people in hiding were arrested.

Historical-Fiction Reflection

The Pins on the Map

An imagined first-person reflection by Otto Frank, looking back thirty years after D-Day.

Read as historical fiction: The events are grounded in the historical record, but these are not Otto Frank's actual words, diary entries, or memoir.
LOOKING BACK FROM 1974

Thirty years have passed since June 6, 1944, yet when I think of that day I do not first picture the beaches of Normandy. I picture a small map in Amsterdam.

We had lived in the Secret Annex for nearly two years. Our world had become a handful of rooms, a staircase, a hidden doorway, the sounds from the offices below, and the news that reached us from outside. The war was enormous, but our lives had become very small. We measured danger in footsteps, voices, doors, and the hours when we had to remain quiet.

June 1944 — Hope

Then came the news of the Allied landings in France. Normandy was far from Amsterdam, but the distance seemed to shrink as we listened. Armies had crossed the Channel. Men were fighting their way onto the coast. The occupation had not ended, but at last there was movement from the west that we could follow.

I marked the Allied progress on a map with pins. It was a small act, but in hiding small things could carry great meaning. A pin moved forward. A town changed hands. A line on a map shifted. Each change suggested that the world outside the Annex was not frozen forever.

Looking back, it is easy to give June 6 a certainty we did not have at the time. We did not know how long the fighting in Normandy would last. We did not know when the Netherlands would be liberated. We certainly did not know what would happen to us. Hope had entered the room, but hope was not the same as safety.

August 1944 — What We Could Not Know

Less than two months later, on August 4, our hiding place was discovered and we were arrested. The map and its pins could not protect us. The armies were moving across Europe, yet our own path led first to Westerbork and then east to Auschwitz.

At Auschwitz I was separated from Edith, Anne, and Margot. I know now that I would never see them again. At the time, I could only wait and hope.

By the winter I was weak and ill. When the SS evacuated most prisoners from Auschwitz as Soviet troops approached, I remained behind in the sick barracks. On January 27, 1945, Soviet soldiers entered the camp. I was liberated at Auschwitz by troops advancing from the east; the western Allied armies that had landed in Normandy were fighting on another front. No single day or army explains how the war in Europe ended.

1945 — Return

When I finally returned to Amsterdam on June 3, 1945, the war in Europe was over. The helpers who had risked their lives for us had survived. My family had not.

During the journey home I had learned that Edith had died in Auschwitz. I still hoped Anne and Margot might return. In July, that hope ended. Women who had been with them in Bergen-Belsen told me that my daughters had died there.

Miep then gave me Anne's papers. She had kept them after our arrest, hoping to return them to Anne. Reading the diary was not like recovering the daughter I had lost. It showed me parts of Anne's mind that I had never fully known. Later, when I learned that she had hoped to publish a book after the war, I decided to help make that possible. Her diary appeared in Dutch in 1947.

Looking Back — History and One Family

So what does D-Day mean to me after thirty years?

It means hope, certainly. The landings in Normandy were a crucial step in the liberation of Western Europe. The soldiers who crossed the Channel helped push Nazi rule back from the west, while other Allied armies fought on other fronts.

But memory asks for more than gratitude. It asks for accuracy. D-Day did not make us safe on June 6. It did not prevent our arrest in August. It did not determine when or how Auschwitz would be liberated. And it did not return my wife or daughters to me.

A military map records advances with lines and pins. A family remembers empty chairs, missing voices, and names that must continue to be spoken. Both views belong to history.

Perhaps that is what I understand most clearly now. Great events matter because they change the lives of individual people. Yet no great event can explain every life by itself. To remember the war truthfully, we must look at the armies and the maps—and also at the people waiting, hiding, surviving, returning, and mourning.

Supporting Historical Context

From Arrest to Return

The opening timeline gives the broad sequence. These details clarify what happened after the hiding place was discovered and what Otto learned after liberation.

Documented

From Arrest to Liberation

  • After the August 4 arrest, the eight people from the Secret Annex were sent first to Westerbork and then to Auschwitz-Birkenau.
  • At Auschwitz-Birkenau, Otto was separated from Edith, Anne, and Margot; it was the last time he saw them.
  • When the SS evacuated most prisoners in January 1945, Otto was too ill to leave. Soviet troops entered Auschwitz on January 27 and liberated the prisoners who remained.

Documented

From Return to Publication

  • During his journey home, Otto learned that Edith had died in Auschwitz. He returned to Amsterdam on June 3, 1945, still hoping Anne and Margot had survived.
  • On July 18, Otto received confirmation that Anne and Margot had died in Bergen-Belsen. Soon afterward, Miep Gies gave him Anne's surviving diary papers.
  • Otto later worked to fulfill Anne's wish to publish a book about life in hiding. Het Achterhuis first appeared in Dutch on June 25, 1947.
Important distinction: D-Day was a crucial step in the liberation of Western Europe, but it did not directly liberate Otto Frank. Soviet troops liberated Auschwitz on January 27, 1945.
